# IOAI 2024 — Past Problems and Competition Retrospective

The first International Olympiad in Artificial Intelligence (IOAI) took place in Budapest, Hungary in August 2024, marking a historic milestone for competitive AI education. Understanding what happened in 2024 — what was tested, what differentiated top performers, and what common mistakes were made — is one of the highest-leverage ways to prepare for future editions.

---

## Section 1: What Is IOAI?

IOAI is a student olympiad modeled on the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) and the International Physics Olympiad (IPhO), but centered entirely on artificial intelligence and machine learning. Each country sends a team of students who compete individually. The competition tests both theoretical understanding of ML and practical skill in solving ML problems under time constraints.

### Historical Context

IOAI 2024 was the first edition, making it simultaneously the most important and the most uncertain competition to study. There was no established "meta" — no prior problems, no known difficulty calibration, no strategies passed down from previous years. This mirrors the early years of IOI or IMO, where participants essentially had to derive best practices from first principles.

Key facts about IOAI 2024:
- Location: Budapest, Hungary
- Date: August 2024
- Format: Two exam components — a theoretical written exam and a practical (computational) exam
- Individual competition: No team scoring; medals awarded per-student
- Countries participating: Approximately 40+ countries in the first year

### How IOAI Differs from Kaggle

Students familiar with Kaggle competitions will notice similarities and critical differences:

| Dimension | Kaggle | IOAI |
|---|---|---|
| Time horizon | Days to months | 4–5 hours |
| Team size | Up to 5 | Individual |
| Resources | Unlimited internet, compute | Controlled environment |
| Metric | Single leaderboard score | Partial credit possible |
| Theory | Not tested | Explicitly tested in exam |

The most important implication: in IOAI, you cannot spend days iterating on feature engineering. Every minute counts. Speed and correctness of your first intuition matter enormously.

### How IOAI Differs from IOI

IOI (International Olympiad in Informatics) tests algorithmic problem-solving: dynamic programming, graph theory, data structures. IOAI overlaps in requiring programming skill but tests an entirely different skill: the ability to apply machine learning techniques effectively to real datasets. You will not be writing Dijkstra's algorithm. You will be loading a CSV, engineering features, training a classifier, and submitting predictions.

## Section 2: Structure of the 2024 Competition

### Theoretical Exam

The theoretical component tests conceptual understanding of machine learning. This is a written exam with no coding. Expect questions on:

- Mathematical foundations: linear algebra (matrix operations, eigenvalues), calculus (gradients, chain rule), probability (Bayes' theorem, distributions)
- ML theory: bias-variance tradeoff, regularization, loss functions, optimization algorithms
- Model mechanics: how does a decision tree split? What is the VC dimension? How does backpropagation work?
- Algorithmic analysis: time/space complexity of training algorithms, convergence guarantees

Example theoretical question style: *"A logistic regression model trained with L2 regularization parameter λ is fit on a dataset. As λ → ∞, what happens to the model's weights and why? What does this imply about the bias-variance tradeoff?"*

This requires a precise mathematical answer, not just intuition.

### Practical Exam

The practical component is where participants solve real ML problems using code. In 2024, participants worked with:

1. **Tabular data classification** — The most common task type. Given a CSV with numerical and categorical features, predict a target class. Evaluated on accuracy or F1-score.
2. **Image classification** — Given a set of labeled images, build a classifier. Limited compute means you likely cannot train deep networks from scratch; pretrained features or shallow models are expected.
3. **Text classification** — Given labeled text samples, classify them into categories. TF-IDF + Logistic Regression is a strong baseline; transformer embeddings if available.
4. **Regression tasks** — Predict a continuous value; evaluated on RMSE or MAE.

Tasks are delivered as datasets with a training split and a test split. Participants submit predictions for the test split.

## Section 3: Key Techniques That Were Crucial

Success in IOAI 2024 required command of a specific toolkit. Not cutting-edge research, but solid, reliable applied ML.

### Data Preprocessing

Garbage in, garbage out. The single highest-ROI skill in practical ML competitions:

- **Missing value handling**: `SimpleImputer` with `median` strategy for numerics, `most_frequent` for categoricals. Never leave NaN values in your data unless the model explicitly handles them (tree-based models often do).
- **Scaling**: `StandardScaler` or `MinMaxScaler` for linear models and neural networks. Tree-based methods (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) are scale-invariant but scaling never hurts.
- **Encoding**: `OneHotEncoder` for low-cardinality categoricals, `OrdinalEncoder` for high-cardinality or ordinal features.
- **Outlier detection**: Check for extreme values with IQR or z-score. Don't blindly remove them — sometimes they are signal.

### Feature Engineering

Features are hypotheses about what drives the target variable. Good feature engineering requires domain reasoning, not just mechanical transformations:

- Log-transform right-skewed numerical features (income, prices, counts)
- Create interaction terms: `feature_A * feature_B` when you suspect multiplicative effects
- Bin continuous variables into buckets if there are threshold effects
- Extract date/time components: hour-of-day, day-of-week, month, is-weekend
- Create ratio features: `value / group_mean` to normalize for group effects

### Model Selection

The correct model depends on the data characteristics:

- **Logistic Regression**: Best first model for classification. Fast, interpretable, strong regularization. Use as your baseline.
- **Random Forest**: Excellent out-of-the-box performance, handles mixed feature types, less sensitive to hyperparameters than gradient boosting.
- **Gradient Boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM / sklearn GBC)**: Typically the strongest tabular model. Requires more tuning but rewards it.
- **MLP**: Good for large tabular datasets with many features. Requires careful scaling and tuning.

### Ensembling

Combining models almost always improves performance. The simplest ensemble is averaging predictions:

- Average the predicted probabilities from multiple models
- Use voting for hard predictions
- Stack models: use predictions of base models as features for a meta-learner

Ensembling works best when models make different errors. A Random Forest and a Gradient Booster trained on the same data will often disagree in useful ways.

### Validation Strategy

This is where many participants failed in 2024. Your local validation score must be a reliable estimate of your test score, or you are flying blind.

- Use `StratifiedKFold` for classification to preserve class balance across folds
- Use 5-fold CV as a minimum; 10-fold for small datasets
- Make sure preprocessing happens inside the fold (not before), to avoid data leakage
- If the test set has a time component, use `TimeSeriesSplit`

## Section 4: A Complete EDA and Baseline Pipeline

This is the code you should be able to write from memory in the first 30 minutes of a practical exam.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, f1_score

# ── 1. Load data ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
train = pd.read_csv("train.csv")
test  = pd.read_csv("test.csv")

TARGET = "label"       # change to actual target column name
ID_COL = "id"          # change to actual ID column if present

X = train.drop(columns=[TARGET, ID_COL], errors="ignore")
y = train[TARGET]
X_test = test.drop(columns=[ID_COL], errors="ignore")

print("Train shape:", X.shape)
print("Test shape: ", X_test.shape)
print("\nTarget distribution:\n", y.value_counts(normalize=True))

# ── 2. EDA ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
print("\nDtypes:\n", X.dtypes.value_counts())
print("\nMissing values:\n", X.isnull().sum()[X.isnull().sum() > 0])
print("\nNumerical summary:\n", X.describe())

# Identify column types
num_cols = X.select_dtypes(include=["number"]).columns.tolist()
cat_cols = X.select_dtypes(exclude=["number"]).columns.tolist()

print(f"\nNumerical: {len(num_cols)} columns")
print(f"Categorical: {len(cat_cols)} columns")
if cat_cols:
    for c in cat_cols:
        print(f"  {c}: {X[c].nunique()} unique values")

# ── 3. Preprocessing pipeline ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
num_pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
    ("scaler", StandardScaler()),
])

cat_pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
    ("encoder", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore", sparse_output=False)),
])

preprocessor = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", num_pipeline, num_cols),
    ("cat", cat_pipeline, cat_cols),
])

# ── 4. Baseline model ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
baseline = Pipeline([
    ("prep", preprocessor),
    ("model", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, C=1.0)),
])

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
scores = cross_val_score(baseline, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="f1_macro")
print(f"\nLogistic Regression CV F1: {scores.mean():.4f} ± {scores.std():.4f}")

# ── 5. Stronger model ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
rf = Pipeline([
    ("prep", preprocessor),
    ("model", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)),
])

scores_rf = cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="f1_macro")
print(f"Random Forest CV F1:       {scores_rf.mean():.4f} ± {scores_rf.std():.4f}")

gb = Pipeline([
    ("prep", preprocessor),
    ("model", GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=200, learning_rate=0.05,
                                          max_depth=4, random_state=42)),
])

scores_gb = cross_val_score(gb, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="f1_macro")
print(f"Gradient Boosting CV F1:   {scores_gb.mean():.4f} ± {scores_gb.std():.4f}")

# ── 6. Fit on full train, generate predictions ────────────────────────────────
best_model = gb   # replace with whichever scored best
best_model.fit(X, y)
predictions = best_model.predict(X_test)

submission = pd.DataFrame({
    ID_COL: test[ID_COL] if ID_COL in test.columns else range(len(test)),
    TARGET: predictions,
})
submission.to_csv("submission.csv", index=False)
print("\nSubmission saved. Shape:", submission.shape)
print(submission.head())
```

This pipeline covers approximately 80% of tabular classification tasks you will encounter. Memorize it. Adapt it during the exam.

## Section 5: Common Mistakes in IOAI 2024

Based on analysis of participant experiences and retrospectives from the first IOAI, these are the most common failure modes:

### Mistake 1: Data Leakage in Preprocessing

The most subtle and damaging error. If you scale or impute your data using statistics computed on the full training set (including validation rows), your CV score will be optimistically biased.

**Wrong:**
```python
# BAD: fit scaler on all training data before splitting
scaler = StandardScaler()
X_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X)  # leaks validation info
cross_val_score(model, X_scaled, y, cv=5)
```

**Right:**
```python
# GOOD: preprocessing is inside the pipeline, applied per-fold
pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("scaler", StandardScaler()),
    ("model", LogisticRegression()),
])
cross_val_score(pipeline, X, y, cv=5)  # scaler fits only on training fold
```

### Mistake 2: Ignoring the Evaluation Metric

Accuracy and F1-macro are not the same. If the competition uses F1-macro on an imbalanced dataset, optimizing for accuracy will produce misleading results and a poor score.

Always confirm the metric in the problem statement. Then confirm that your `cross_val_score` uses the correct `scoring=` parameter.

Common metrics:
- `"accuracy"` — proportion correct (misleading on imbalanced data)
- `"f1_macro"` — macro-averaged F1 across classes (preferred for imbalanced multi-class)
- `"f1_weighted"` — class-frequency-weighted F1
- `"roc_auc"` — AUC-ROC for binary classification
- `"neg_mean_squared_error"` — for regression (negated so higher is better)

### Mistake 3: Not Reading the Data Description

Problem statements in IOAI include metadata about features. Ignoring this means missing domain knowledge that could dramatically improve your model. If the problem says "feature X is a date" and you treat it as a raw integer, you lose the ability to extract day-of-week or seasonality.

### Mistake 4: Over-Tuning on a Small Dataset

With fewer than 1,000 training samples, aggressive hyperparameter tuning will overfit. The correct response to a small dataset is:
- Strong regularization (high `C` in logistic regression means less regularization — use lower values)
- Simpler models (logistic regression, shallow trees)
- More cross-validation folds (use 10-fold or leave-one-out)
- Data augmentation if applicable (NLP, CV)

### Mistake 5: Running Out of Time on Basics

Some participants spent so long on EDA that they never submitted a competitive model. The recommended time allocation for a 4-hour practical:

- 0:00–0:20 — Read problem statement, understand metric, look at raw data
- 0:20–0:45 — EDA: shapes, dtypes, missing values, class distribution
- 0:45–1:15 — Build and evaluate baseline pipeline
- 1:15–2:30 — Feature engineering, model selection, hyperparameter tuning
- 2:30–3:30 — Ensemble top models, evaluate on CV
- 3:30–4:00 — Final submission, sanity check, backup submission

Always submit the baseline before doing anything else. A submitted baseline guarantees a non-zero score. Optimization can happen after.

## Section 6: What Differentiated Top Performers

Top participants in IOAI 2024 shared several characteristics:

### Clean, Reproducible Pipelines

Top performers set random seeds everywhere (`random_state=42` in every model, `np.random.seed(42)` at the top). They structured their code so that the full pipeline could be re-run from scratch in under 30 seconds. This meant they could confidently iterate without worrying that stochastic variation was misleading them.

### Strong Validation Strategy

Top performers matched their validation scheme to the data structure. If the test set was from a different time period than training, they used time-aware splitting. If classes were imbalanced, they used stratified splitting. The key insight: **your local CV score is only useful if it correlates with the true test score.**

### Rapid Baseline Then Iterate

The fastest-scoring participants got a working submission in the first 45 minutes and then spent the remaining time improving it. This is psychologically and strategically optimal: you are always improving on something concrete, not aiming at a blank page.

### Domain Knowledge Application

The best feature engineers thought about the problem domain before writing code. For a tabular dataset about loan default prediction: "default rate varies by age group, income level, loan-to-income ratio, and time-to-repayment" — these are hypotheses that lead directly to feature engineering decisions.

### Knowing When to Stop Tuning

Top performers recognized when their CV score had plateaued and pivoted to ensembling or post-processing instead of endless hyperparameter search. Diminishing returns on tuning set in quickly.

## Section 7: Lessons for Future IOAI Editions

The first IOAI established several patterns that are likely to persist:

1. **Tabular classification is the foundation.** Every serious IOAI participant must be comfortable with the full sklearn tabular pipeline: load, EDA, preprocess, model, validate, submit.

2. **The metric matters as much as the model.** You can have the best model in the room and still lose if you are optimizing the wrong metric.

3. **Theoretical knowledge shows up in practical work.** Understanding why L2 regularization prevents overfitting helps you choose the right regularization strength. Theory and practice are not separate.

4. **Speed is a competitive advantage.** Two participants with equal knowledge, where one can execute their pipeline twice as fast, will have meaningfully different final scores.

5. **Practice on real datasets.** UCI ML Repository, Kaggle datasets, and scikit-learn's built-in datasets all provide good practice material. The skill of going from raw data to CV-validated model in under an hour is trainable.

## Section 8: Recommended Practice Problems

To prepare for IOAI-style practical problems, work through these exercises in timed conditions (treat each as a 90-minute sprint):

### Exercise 1: Titanic-Style Binary Classification
Dataset: Any binary survival/churn/default prediction dataset with mixed feature types.
Goal: Get a cross-validated F1 score > 0.80 in 90 minutes.
Constraint: No internet, sklearn only.

### Exercise 2: Imbalanced Multi-Class Classification
Dataset: A dataset with 5+ classes and class imbalance (e.g., intrusion detection, document categorization).
Goal: Maximize F1-macro.
Key challenge: Deal with class imbalance — try `class_weight="balanced"` in your models.

### Exercise 3: Feature-Rich Tabular Regression
Dataset: Any regression dataset with 20+ features, some categorical.
Goal: Minimize RMSE using CV.
Key challenge: Log-transform the target if it is skewed.

```python
# Quick template for regression
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error
import numpy as np

# If target is skewed, log-transform it
y_log = np.log1p(y)  # inverse: np.expm1(predictions)

pipeline_reg = Pipeline([
    ("prep", preprocessor),
    ("model", GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=200, learning_rate=0.05,
                                         max_depth=4, random_state=42)),
])

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
scores = cross_val_score(pipeline_reg, X, y_log, cv=5,
                         scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
rmse = np.sqrt(-scores.mean())
print(f"CV RMSE (log scale): {rmse:.4f}")
```

## Summary

IOAI 2024 established that competitive AI olympiad success requires:

- A reliable, memorized baseline pipeline for tabular classification and regression
- Deep understanding of data preprocessing: imputation, scaling, encoding
- Strong validation discipline: correct CV strategy, correct metric, no leakage
- Fast execution: get a working submission within the first hour
- Theoretical grounding: understanding why your techniques work, not just how to call them

The students who medal at future IOAI editions will be those who have internalized these skills at the level of muscle memory — not students who vaguely understand the concepts but have to think carefully about each line of code during a timed exam.
